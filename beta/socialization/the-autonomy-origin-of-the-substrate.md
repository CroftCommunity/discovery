**What this is.** A short origin story for the answer to "why aren't you reinventing the wheel?" It explains
why an open, federated social substrate exists for Croft to build on — and why that substrate is
structurally, not just technically, aligned with a non-extractive product. The human-facing companion to the
ecosystem fact (the verified `Bluesky PBC` / `AT Protocol` rows in `../../alpha/ECOSYSTEM.md`) and to the
peer-standing → cooperative-form argument in `../philosophy/`.

## The story

A large social platform once commissioned a project to build not a feature, but a *protocol for the
internet* — an open, decentralized standard meant to solve platform lock-in, the trap where users cannot
take their content or social graph with them when they leave.

The person recruited to lead it set one condition that turned out to be everything: **significant autonomy.**
True decentralization could not thrive under a centralized corporate giant, so the project was spun out into
an independent entity — a **Public Benefit Corporation** — legally separated from its funder. The parent
company provided initial money and surrendered control; the project was freed from quarterly earnings, board
shifts, and management fads.

Then the parent was bought and gutted — mass layoffs, degraded stability, a rebrand into something
unrecognizable. For almost any project living inside that company, the acquisition would have been fatal. But
the autonomy secured months earlier had *firewalled* the project: it remained a separate, functioning entity
while its birthplace was dismantled.

That autonomy is why the substrate is real. The team delivered the original promise — an open protocol
(**the AT Protocol**) built for federation rather than central control:

- **User portability** — you own your identity and data and can move between servers without losing your
  followers or history.
- **Decentralized moderation** — communities set their own rules and filters; no single point of failure.
- **Infrastructure at scale** — a standardized open protocol on which applications interact with the network
  natively.

So serious applications can be built on open ground, at meaningful population scale, without reinventing the
wheel. The irony: the company that tried to *own* the future of communication lost the one piece of it that
turned out to matter — the open protocol it let walk out the door.

## Why it matters for Croft

- **The wheel already exists, and it is open.** Croft's ponds/pads build on this substrate (ECOSYSTEM
  "build-on, public path"); the corpus has proven the substrate live (the sovereign-appview / atproto-
  architecture intakes; PR #4/#6). The origin story is *why* that ground is available and open rather than
  another walled garden.
- **The structural-autonomy move rhymes with the cooperative thesis.** The lesson isn't "a benevolent
  founder made good choices"; it's that a *legal structure* insulated the mission from extraction. That is
  the same shape as Croft's non-extractive/cooperative argument (`../philosophy/peer-standing-and-the-
  cooperative-form.md`): mission survives when the structure, not the goodwill, protects it.
- **It answers the "why not just use X?" objection honestly.** Croft is not competing to rebuild a protocol;
  it is building the human-facing garden on top of one that already carries the open properties Croft needs.
